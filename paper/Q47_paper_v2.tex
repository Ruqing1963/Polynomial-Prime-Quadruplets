\begin{abstract}
We study prime values and prime constellations of $Q_q(n) = n^q - (n-1)^q$, the degree-$(q-1)$ analogues of the cuban primes, with emphasis on $q = 47$. Every prime factor of $Q_q(n)$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod{2q}$, and each such prime excludes exactly $q-1$ residue classes of $n$; from this we compute the Bateman--Horn constants $C_k(q)$ of the $k$-tuples $Q_q(n),\dots,Q_q(n+k-1)$ for ten exponents. The data agree with Bateman--Horn at every scale we tested. For $Q_{47}$ we count 18,473,624 primes for $n \le 2\times10^9$ (predicted 18,464,550), 2,393 prime quadruplets for $n < 8\times10^{11}$ (predicted 2,404.3) and 21 quintuplets (predicted 17.9). In windows at $n\approx10^{20}$ and $n\approx 4.8\times10^{21}$ we find quadruplets of 922 and 1000 digits; for eight of the 1000-digit quadruplets all four members carry ECPP primality certificates. A sieve-and-PRP search at 200,000 digits found
\[
Q_{47}(586135293385\cdot10^{4336}+1647),
\]
a probable prime of exactly 200,000 digits that passes Fermat tests to bases 3, 5, 7, 11 and independent strong base-11 and BPSW tests with GMP; we believe it is the largest known prime-candidate value of $Q_{47}$. The large differences between exponents in quadruplet frequency are explained by the constants $C_4(q)$, which are governed by the smallest prime $p\equiv1\pmod q$: when $2q+1$ is prime, that single prime cuts $C_4$ by an order of magnitude. Whether $q$ is a safe prime plays no role. A previously reported correlation between quadruplet positions and Riemann zeros disappears completely after detrending.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Changes from Version 1}
Version 1 (January 2026) reported 15 quadruplets of $Q_{47}$ below $2\times10^9$, attributed their abundance to $q=47$ being a safe prime through an ``interference potential'' $I(q)=d(q-1)-2$, and reported a correlation $r=0.994$ between quadruplet positions and Riemann zeros. Version 2 replaces that framework with an explicit Bateman--Horn analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item the constants $C_k(q)$, $k\le 6$, are computed (Section~\ref{sec:constants}); they predict the observed counts, and the variation between exponents comes from the smallest prime $p\equiv 1 \pmod q$, not from the safe-prime property (Section~\ref{sec:seven});
\item the $Q_{47}$ census is extended to $8\times10^{11}$, with quintuplets (Section~\ref{sec:census});
\item quadruplets at 922 and 1000 digits, with ECPP certificates, and a 200,000-digit probable prime are added (Sections~\ref{sec:deep}, \ref{sec:prp});
\item the Riemann-zero correlation is re-examined with a null model and a detrended test, and is not significant (Section~\ref{sec:riemann});
\item the subgroup lattice of $(\mathbb{Z}/47\mathbb{Z})^\ast$, drawn in v1 as a chain $\{1\}\subset H_2\subset H_{23}$, is in fact a diamond ($2\nmid 23$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
For a prime $q$ let
\begin{equation}
Q_q(n) = n^q - (n-1)^q = \sum_{j=0}^{q-1} n^j (n-1)^{q-1-j},
\end{equation}
an irreducible polynomial of degree $q-1$ with leading coefficient $q$ and the symmetry $Q_q(1-n)=Q_q(n)$. For $q=3$, $Q_3(n)=3n^2-3n+1$ and its prime values are the cuban primes. We call $n,n+1,\dots,n+k-1$ a \emph{prime $k$-tuple} (doublet, triplet, quadruplet, quintuplet for $k=2,3,4,5$) if $Q_q(n),\dots,Q_q(n+k-1)$ are all prime.

The Bateman--Horn conjecture~\cite{BH} predicts the number of such $k$-tuples with $2\le n\le Y$ to be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BH}
N_k(Y) \sim C_k(q)\int_2^Y \frac{dt}{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\ln Q_q(t+i)},\qquad
C_k(q) = \prod_p \frac{1-\omega_k(p)/p}{(1-1/p)^k},
\end{equation}
where $\omega_k(p)$ is the number of residues $n \bmod p$ with $p \mid Q_q(n)Q_q(n+1)\cdots Q_q(n+k-1)$. This paper computes $C_k(q)$, tests~\eqref{eq:BH} on large data sets, and reports constellations and single primes of $Q_{47}$ at sizes up to 200,000 digits.

\paragraph{Related work.} Since $n-(n-1)=1$, $Q_q(n)=\Phi_q(n,n-1)$ is a value of the homogeneous cyclotomic polynomial. The congruence $p\equiv1\pmod q$ for its prime factors is the classical theory of primitive divisors of $a^q-b^q$~\cite{Zsig,BV}. Prime values of the family have been tabulated for small $q$: the cuban primes ($q=3$)~\cite{Cuban}, the primes $n^5-(n-1)^5$~\cite{A121616}, and the least $b$ with $\Phi_m(b,b-1)$ prime~\cite{A250201}. We know of no census of consecutive prime values ($k\ge2$) for $q\ge5$ apart from the author's earlier preprints on $q=47$. The Bateman--Horn conjecture~\cite{BH} is surveyed in~\cite{AFG}. Numerical tests go back to Shanks for $n^2+1$~\cite{Shanks}. The singular products converge slowly and only conditionally; Shanks and Lal accelerated them with Dedekind zeta values~\cite{ShanksLal}. We use plain truncated products (Section~\ref{sec:constants}), which is adequate at the precision needed here.

This paper extends the author's earlier preprints on $Q_{47}$: the constant $C_1(47)\approx8.68$~\cite{Chen1}, the census of primes and constellations for $n\le2\times10^9$~\cite{Chen2}, the quadruplet census to $2\times10^{11}$~\cite{Chen3}, and the Bateman--Horn verification to $8\times10^{11}$ with the 1000-digit quadruplets~\cite{BHver}. The quadruplet counts given here (749 below $2\times10^{11}$ and 2,393 below $8\times10^{11}$, each one confirmed by BPSW) supersede the earlier figures of 742 and 2,359, which did not yet include quadruplets found in later sweeps. New here are:
the constants $C_k(q)$ for ten exponents and the explanation of their spread;
the complete recount of seven exponents, which corrects Version~1;
the control search;
21 quintuplets;
the 200,000-digit probable prime;
the detrended re-examination of the Riemann-zero correlation.

\section{Arithmetic of $Q_q$}\label{sec:arith}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:roots}
Let $q$ be an odd prime and $p$ a prime.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $p\mid Q_q(n)$ then $p\equiv 1 \pmod{2q}$. In particular $Q_q(n)$ is odd, and its smallest possible prime factor is the least prime $\equiv 1\pmod{2q}$ (for $q=47$: $283$).
\item For $p\equiv 1\pmod q$, $p\mid Q_q(n)$ holds exactly on the $q-1$ classes $n\equiv (1-u)^{-1}\pmod p$, $u^q=1\ne u$; these come in pairs $r,\,1-r$.
\item $Q_q(n)\equiv 1 \pmod{\kappa(q)}$ for all $n$, where $\kappa(q)=\prod_{(\ell-1)\mid(q-1)}\ell$ is the denominator of the Bernoulli number $B_{q-1}$; for $q=47$, $\kappa=2\cdot3\cdot47=282$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1)--(2): if $p\mid Q_q(n)$ then $p\nmid n$ (otherwise $Q_q(n)\equiv 1$) and $u=(n-1)/n$ satisfies $u^q\equiv 1$, $u\not\equiv1$, so $u$ has order $q$ in $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$, $q\mid p-1$, and $p$ is odd since $Q_q(n)$ is odd. Conversely $n=(1-u)^{-1}$ recovers $n$, and $u\mapsto u^{-1}$ maps $n$ to $1-n$. (3) follows from Fermat's little theorem; see~\cite{BC}.
\end{proof}

Hence $\omega_1(p)=q-1$ if $p\equiv1\pmod q$ and $\omega_1(p)=0$ otherwise, and $\omega_k(p)$ is the size of the union of the root set $R_p$ and its translates $R_p-1,\dots,R_p-(k-1)$. Over $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_q)$ one has $Q_q(n)=N_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_q)/\mathbb{Q}}\bigl(n-\zeta_q(n-1)\bigr)$, which is the source of these congruences.

\paragraph{Subgroup lattice.} Since $(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^\ast$ is cyclic of order $q-1$, its subgroup lattice is the divisor lattice of $q-1$. For $q=47$ it has four elements $\{1\}\subset H_2,\,H_{23}\subset(\mathbb{Z}/47\mathbb{Z})^\ast$ with $H_2$ and $H_{23}$ incomparable. Version~1 used $I(q)=d(q-1)-2$, the number of proper non-trivial subgroups, as a predictor of clustering; Section~\ref{sec:constants} shows that it does not predict $C_4(q)$.

\section{The Bateman--Horn Constants}\label{sec:constants}
The product in~\eqref{eq:BH} converges only conditionally: primes $p\not\equiv1\pmod q$ contribute $(1-1/p)^{-k}>1$, and primes $p\equiv1\pmod q$, of relative density $1/(q-1)$, contribute $1-\omega_k(p)/p$ with $\omega_k(p)\approx k(q-1)$. We evaluated the partial products over all $p\le M$ with $M=2\times10^6,\,2\times10^7,\,2\times10^8$; between $M=2\times10^6$ and $2\times10^8$ the values change by less than $0.5\%$ for every $q$ and $k$. \paragraph{What determines $C_4(q)$.} The constants vary by a factor of 30 across exponents of similar size. The variation is dominated by the least prime $p_1\equiv1\pmod q$. If $p_1=2q+1$, the $4(q-1)$ excluded classes nearly exhaust $\mathbb{Z}/p_1\mathbb{Z}$ (e.g.\ $\omega_4(83)=76$ for $q=41$), and the local factor is about $0.1$. If $2q+1$ is composite, then $p_1\ge 4q+1$ and the local factor is $0.35$--$0.7$. Being a safe prime is unrelated: $q=23$ is both safe and Sophie Germain and has the smallest $C_4$; $q=47$ is safe but not Sophie Germain ($95=5\cdot19$). The interference potential $I(q)$ of v1 is likewise uncorrelated with $C_4$: $I=2$ for $q=23$ and $47$, $I=6$ for both $q=41$ ($C_4=1263$) and $q=71$ ($C_4=8532$).

\section{Data and Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{Exhaustive range.} For seven exponents $q\in\{37,41,43,47,53,61,71\}$, $Q_q(n)$ was tested for $n\le 2\times10^9$ with Miller--Rabin tests~\cite{Miller} (v1).
\paragraph{$Q_{47}$ quadruplet census.} Segmented sweeps over $n<8\times10^{11}$ used a sieve on the excluded classes of Proposition~\ref{prop:roots} followed by probable-prime tests of $Q_{47}(n),\dots,Q_{47}(n+3)$. All quintuplets were re-checked with BPSW tests~\cite{BPSW} in GMP.
\paragraph{Independent recount.} To check the census below $2\times10^9$ independently, we wrote a second program (\texttt{quad\_census.py}): a segmented sieve with all primes $p\equiv1\pmod q$, $p\le3\times10^7$, applied to the four shifts, a strong base-2 test of each survivor, and BPSW confirmation of every quadruplet found. This program was run for $q=37,41,43,47$. For $q=53,61,71$ the recount comes from separate full sweeps of $1\le n\le2\times10^9$: a sieve with $p\le5\times10^6$ in blocks of $10^7$, followed by Miller--Rabin tests. These sweeps skip the last three starts of each block, so those $600$ starts per exponent were tested separately (\texttt{patch\_titan\_gaps.py}); none is a quadruplet. Every reported quadruplet was confirmed by BPSW.
\paragraph{Control search.} For eight exponents, \texttt{control\_search.py} counts primes and $k$-tuples for $n\le10^7$ after sieving with $p\le10^5$.
\paragraph{Large windows.} Windows of consecutive $n$ starting at $10^{20}$ and at
\[n_0=4797791281515011781318\]
(chosen so that $Q_{47}$ has 1000 digits) were sieved on the excluded classes and searched with probable-prime tests. Quadruplet members at 1000 digits were certified with Primo~4.3.3~\cite{Primo}.

All scripts, logs and certificates are in the repository listed under Data Availability.

\section{Single Primes and Tuples of $Q_{47}$ up to $2\times10^9$}
Up to $n=2\times10^9$ there are 18,473,624 primes $Q_{47}(n)$. Bateman--Horn with $C_1=8.683$ predicts 18,464,550 ($O/E=1.0005$). There are 176,896 doublets and 1,779 triplets (overlapping runs counted), against predictions of 176,780 ($O/E=1.0007$) and 1,752 ($O/E=1.015$, $z=+0.65$). The prime list consists of the author's search files~\cite{Chen2}. One window, $1{,}399{,}874{,}855\le n\le1{,}399{,}880{,}000$, was missing from them and was re-tested; it adds 53 primes (\texttt{patch\_prime\_gaps.py}). No other run of more than 2000 consecutive $n$ without a prime occurs.

The author also searched two windows near $10^{44}$, where $Q_{47}(n)$ has 2026 digits. In $10^{44}<n\le10^{44}+10^6$ there are 1,870 primes against a prediction of 1,861.6 ($z=+0.19$). In a window of $10^7$ consecutive $n$ starting at $n\approx1.004\times10^{44}$ there are 18,715 primes against 18,614 ($z=+0.74$). These windows contain 5 and 24 doublets against $3.6$ and $35.8$ predicted, and no triplets. Both windows were rescanned independently (\texttt{rescan\_window.py}). Every $n$ was sieved by all $p\equiv1\pmod{47}$ with $p\le10^8$; this left 264,775 and 2,647,699 candidates. These were tested as Fermat base-3 probable primes with PFGW, and each hit was confirmed by BPSW. The rescan reproduces both lists exactly: no prime is missing, and none fails to be confirmed. \section{Constellations at 922 and 1000 Digits}\label{sec:deep}

\section{A 200,000-Digit $Q_{47}$ Probable Prime}\label{sec:prp}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:prp}
Let $n^\ast = 586135293385\cdot10^{4336} + 1647$, an integer of 4,348 digits. Then
\[
P^\ast = Q_{47}(n^\ast) = (586135293385\cdot 10^{4336}+1647)^{47} - (586135293385\cdot 10^{4336}+1646)^{47}

has exactly 200,000 decimal digits (664,383 bits) and is a probable prime: it passes the tests in Table~\ref{tab:prp}.

To our knowledge $P^\ast$ is the largest known probable prime of the form $n^{47}-(n-1)^{47}$.

\subsection{Search}
Since $Q_{47}(n)=47n^{46}(1+O(1/n))$, 200,000 digits require $\log_{10}n\approx(200000-\log_{10}47)/46\approx4347.79$. We wrote $n=a_0\cdot10^{4336}+t$ with $a_0=586135293385$, the least 12-digit prefix for which $Q_{47}$ has 200,000 digits, and $0\le t<1.5\times10^5$. Sieving with all primes $p\equiv1\pmod{94}$ up to $2\times10^{10}$ (46 classes each, Proposition~\ref{prop:roots}) left $30{,}809$ candidates ($20.5\%$). These were split into eight equal slices and tested with PFGW in parallel; $P^\ast$ ($t=1647$) was the 341st candidate of the first slice.

\subsection{Comparison with Bateman--Horn}
For candidates free of prime factors $\le B$, the probability of primality is about
\[
\frac{e^\gamma\ln B}{\ln Q_{47}(n)}=\frac{1.781\cdot\ln(2\times10^{10})}{200000\,\ln10}\approx \frac{1}{10{,}900};
\]
the constant $C_1$ cancels after conditioning on the sieve. The window should contain about $2.8$ probable primes. When $P^\ast$ was found, between $2.7\times10^3$ and $3.2\times10^3$ candidates had been tested, for an expected $0.25$--$0.30$ probable primes and a Poisson probability of $22$--$26\%$ of at least one. The discovery is consistent with Bateman--Horn.

\subsection{Primality status}
$P^\ast$ is a probable prime, not a proven prime. An $N-1$ proof~\cite{BLS} needs a factored part of $P^\ast-1$ of about one third of its size. With $m=n^\ast-1$, the Cauchy--Mirimanoff factorization for $47\equiv5\pmod6$ gives
\[
P^\ast-1=(m+1)^{47}-m^{47}-1=47\,m\,(m+1)\,(m^2+m+1)\,E_{47}(m),\qquad \deg E_{47}=42,
\]
so even complete factorizations of $m$, $m+1$ and $m^2+m+1$ would supply about $17{,}400$ of the 200,000 digits ($8.7\%$). ECPP proofs have so far been completed only below $10^5$ digits. $P^\ast$ should therefore be reported as a probable prime, for example to the PRP Top Records list~\cite{PRPtop}.

\section{Exploratory Note on Quadruplet Positions and Riemann Zeros}\label{sec:riemann}
Version~1 reported that the starting points $n_1<\dots<n_{15}$ of Table~\ref{tab:quad} satisfy $n_k^{1/2.74}\approx c\,\gamma_k$ with $r=0.994$, where $\gamma_k$ is the $k$-th ordinate of a zero of $\zeta(s)$, and derived an ``effective modulus'' $q_{\rm eff}=e^{2.74}$ from the fitted exponent. We re-examined this in two ways.

\paragraph{Null model.} Two increasing sequences correlate strongly whatever their origin. For $2\times10^4$ sets of 15 positions drawn uniformly from $[10^6,2\times10^9]$ and sorted, the best correlation over power transforms $n^{1/a}$, $a\in[1,5]$, has median $0.985$ and 95th percentile $0.994$; $r\ge0.994$ occurs in about $5$--$6\%$ of sets.

\paragraph{Detrended test.} We removed the smooth trend on both sides: $a_k=N_4(n_k)-(k-\tfrac12)$ with $N_4$ from~\eqref{eq:BH}, and $b_k=\bar N(\gamma_k)-(k-\tfrac12)$ with $\bar N(T)=\frac{T}{2\pi}\ln\frac{T}{2\pi e}+\frac78$. The correlation of the residuals is $r=-0.0003$ (Spearman $0.09$), with permutation $p=0.999$ (Figure~\ref{fig:resid}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_residuals.png}
\caption{Detrended residuals of quadruplet positions ($a_k$) and Riemann zero ordinates ($b_k$). Left: residuals by index. Right: no relation between them.}\label{fig:resid}
\end{figure}

The quadruplet positions follow the Bateman--Horn trend, and the zero ordinates follow the Riemann--von Mangoldt trend; beyond these trends there is no relation. The fitted exponent $2.74$, and with it $q_{\rm eff}$ and the ``$q/3$ rule'' of v1, describe how two smooth curves can be matched over a short range and carry no arithmetic information. We withdraw these claims.

\section{Discussion}
At every scale tested---single primes to $2\times10^9$, quadruplets to $8\times10^{11}$, quintuplets, and windows at 922 and 1000 digits---the counts for $Q_{47}$ agree with Bateman--Horn within ordinary Poisson fluctuation. The abundance of $Q_{47}$ quadruplets relative to other exponents is a property of the singular series: $C_4(47)$ is large because the smallest prime that can divide $Q_{47}(n)$ is $283$, while for $q=41$ and $q=53$ the primes $83$ and $107$ remove almost every residue class. A useful rule of thumb follows: exponents $q$ for which $2q+1$ is prime are poor sources of constellations of $Q_q$.

\paragraph{Open problems.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A primality certificate for $P^\ast$, or a method using the cyclotomic structure of $Q_{47}$ to supply the factored part a proof would need.
\item Completion of the 200,000-digit window (2.8 probable primes expected).
\item Certificates for quadruplets 9--11 of Table~\ref{tab:1000}, and a 1000-digit quintuplet (roughly $(C_5/C_4)/\ln Q\approx 4\times10^{-3}$ quintuplets per quadruplet at that size).
\item A closed-form estimate of $C_k(q)$ in terms of the primes $p\equiv1\pmod q$ below a few multiples of $q$.
\end{enumerate}
